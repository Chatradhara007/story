Chapter One

The storm reached Kell Pass on Friday evening, and by dark the rescue hut was full. Anselm Voss, the keeper, counted heads by lamplight: four walkers, two skiers, a nurse and a doctor. He was fifty-two, with a thick grey beard and a stiff left leg that dragged a little on the floorboards, a souvenir of a fall on the north face twenty years before.

The nurse was Mira Tolland, thirty-one years old, with short dark hair and brown eyes that she kept narrowed against the glare of the lamp. She had come up with the doctor to look at a climber's frostbitten hands. A red scarf hung from her pocket, the only bright thing in the room.

The doctor was Hartnoll, a large and cheerful man of sixty who insisted on being called Pell. He had brought a bag of medicines, a flask of plum brandy and an endless supply of terrible jokes. By the stove he sat down with the youngest climber, a nineteen-year-old named Jonas Reyes, who had been hiking the high route alone.

"Left-handed," Pell said, watching Jonas hold his tea. "My daughter is the same. It means you are either very clever or very stubborn."

"Both," said Jonas, and grinned.

Anselm hung the radio on its hook beside the door, a battered grey set that connected the hut to the valley station, and tested it. Through the crackle, a voice answered: the pass would be closed until Monday at the earliest.

"Then we are all guests until Monday," Anselm said. He banked the stove and put a pot of soup on to heat.

Outside the wind rose to a long, low howl. Snow hissed against the shutters, and the whole hut seemed to lean into the dark. Mira sat close to the stove with her hands around a mug, listening to it. She had worked in mountain clinics for nine years, and she did not like the sound.

"It will hold," Anselm said, as if she had asked. "It has held for a hundred years."

Nobody answered him. The lamp swung on its chain, and the soup began to bubble.
