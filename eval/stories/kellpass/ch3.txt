Chapter Three

By Sunday morning the survivors had settled into a routine. Anselm kept the stove alive, Mira rationed the food, and everyone avoided looking at the corner where the tarpaulin lay.

Dr. Hartnoll came in from the porch stamping snow from his boots, carrying a pan of melted ice. "Soup is nearly ready," he said cheerfully, and ladled out five bowls with another of his dreadful jokes. Nobody laughed, but Jonas smiled into his bowl.

Mira, thirty-six years old and exhausted, sat beside the window and tried to read the sky. Her grey eyes were red-rimmed from lack of sleep. She remembered her first winter in the clinics, at twenty-two, when she had been so sure that she knew everything.

Anselm took the radio down from its hook and switched it on. After a minute, a faint voice came through the static. He spoke into the microphone, gave the hut's name and the number of people inside, and asked for a helicopter. The valley station promised one as soon as the cloud lifted.

"Tomorrow, if we're lucky," Anselm said, hanging the radio back up.

Jonas had been restless all morning. At noon he pulled on his boots, stood up, and ran out across the ridge to look for the helicopter's landing site, leaping over the drifts like a deer. He came back at a jog twenty minutes later, flushed and laughing, to say that the ridge was clear.

Anselm limped across the room on his right leg, the one that had been stiff since the fall on the north face, and put a hand on the boy's shoulder. His beard, white with frost from the porch, made him look like a figure from an old story.

"We will be all right," he said. "We have a plan now."

That night the stars came out above Kell Pass, hard and bright. In the stove, a last log shifted, and somewhere far below, so faint that Mira thought she imagined it, a dog barked once in the valley.
