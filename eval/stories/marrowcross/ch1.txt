Chapter One: The Inn at Marrow Cross

The Anchor and Key stood at the end of the harbour wall at Marrow Cross, and on the first Monday of October it had exactly one guest. Nell Harker, thirty-four years old, ran the inn with her younger sister Maud and their grandfather Gideon, who was eighty-one and still insisted on sitting in the best chair by the fire.

Nell was a small woman with grey eyes and a way of looking at a room as if she were counting it. Maud was her opposite: tall, loud, black-haired, and never in the kitchen when she was needed.

The one guest was a boatbuilder named Tobias Wren, who had arrived in the afternoon rain with a canvas bag and a long scar across the back of his left hand. He had come, he said, to look at Gideon's boat, the Curlew, which lay on the slip below the inn with her hull painted blue.

"She's sound," Gideon told him, "but she wants a younger man's hands."

Nell gave Tobias the room over the kitchen. Because the inn had been robbed once, years ago, she also showed him the big brass key to the cellar, which hung on a nail by the cellar door and which nobody but the family was allowed to touch. It was the only key the cellar had ever had, and the lock was too old to be replaced.

At supper Tobias ate three helpings of fish stew and asked sensible questions about the tides. Gideon, pleased to have an audience, told the story of the winter the harbour froze, which he told every night of his life. Maud rolled her eyes and went out to smoke on the wall.

Later, alone in the kitchen, Nell counted the cutlery twice. She was not afraid of Tobias. She was afraid of the sea, which had taken her father when she was a girl, and which had been very quiet for a week.

On the wall outside, Maud's cigarette glowed and went out. Somewhere beyond the harbour mouth, the swell began to lift.
