Chapter Three

The market in the valley town was held every Saturday, and on the second Saturday of October the orchard always sold its first crates of Bramleys there. Dominic was up before six. He wheeled the crates out of the shed and loaded them into the back of the blue truck, then started the engine and let it warm up in the yard.

Mr. Osei came across the field, as he had done every Saturday for forty years, with his cap pulled down against the wind. He hoisted a crate onto the tailgate with a grunt. "Heavier than last year," he said, and winked at Iris.

Iris, thirty-three years old that spring, took a basket and a stepladder and went to finish the top branches of the old tree by the gate. She climbed with both hands, one on the rungs and one on the fruit, and her blue eyes squinted against the low sun. The cast on her wrist had been itching for days, but she barely noticed it.

When she came down, Fenna was waiting with a flask of tea. "Mr. Hale rang again," she said. "He asked whether we had thought about his letter."

"What did you tell him?"

"That the apples come first."

At the market, they sold out of Bramleys by ten. A woman in a green coat bought three crates for a pie festival, and a boy bought a single apple with the last of his pocket money. Dominic, who had been growing a beard since the funeral, grinned at every customer and counted the coins into a tin.

On the drive home, Iris thought of the day she had left the valley at twenty-six, sure that she would never come back. She thought of the red barn, and of the two of them lying on the roof, and of Mr. Hale's careful letter on the kitchen table.

At the top of the lane, the lights of the house were already on. Biscuit came limping down to meet them, his grey muzzle wet with rain.
