Chapter Two

They buried Mr. Osei on Tuesday under a low white sky. The whole valley came, and the chapel was too small, so people stood in the churchyard with their hats in their hands. Dominic carried the coffin with five other men, and afterwards he stood by the wall rubbing the old scar through his right eyebrow, as he always did when he was upset.

On Thursday the two of them drove the blue truck up to the top field to check the last rows of Bramleys before the picking began. Halfway up the track a roe deer leapt out of the hedge. Dominic swung the wheel, the truck slid on the wet clay, and it rolled slowly onto its side into the ditch.

Neither of them was badly hurt. Iris had thrown out her right hand to save herself, and the wrist snapped under her. They sat in the long grass while Dominic swore, and she held the arm against her chest and tried not to be sick.

The doctor in town set the wrist that afternoon and put it in a cast up to the elbow. "Six weeks," he said. "No lifting, no climbing, and keep it dry."

The mechanic, a taciturn man named Pruitt, towed the truck down to his yard on Friday morning and walked slowly around it. The front axle had snapped clean through and the frame was bent. "It will never run again," he said. "I would not put a spare part into it. The scrapyard will give you forty pounds."

Dominic signed the paper without arguing. Afterwards he sat on the wall outside Pruitt's yard for a long time.

That evening, Fenna set the offer letter from Mr. Hale on the kitchen table, between the teapot and the bread. It was for a great deal of money. Iris read it with her good hand flat on the page.

"What do you want to do?" Dominic asked his aunt.

"I want to see the apples picked first," Fenna said. "After that, we will decide, all three of us. I will not be hurried by a man in a clean car."

Outside, the rain began again, softly, on the grey roof of the barn.
